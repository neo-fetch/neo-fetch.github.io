South Bengaluru, a Stove, and Old War Songs

By Mayank Deshpande · 25 Apr 2026

↳ Pre-dawn

This isn't a tutorial. There's no architecture diagram, no agent graph, no Python snippet. These are liner notes — about a 26-year-old's life in south Bengaluru, in a year that, by any honest reading of the news, has been louder than most.

About leaving a 1BHK that I could afford but couldn't fill. About cooking, which sounds like a small thing until you live without it. About work I'm grateful for, an MBA I'm preparing for, friends I keep up with imperfectly, and the records I keep returning to when the rest of it gets heavy.

If you came for code, the next track will have it. This one is just a person, in a room, with a stove and a playlist.

↳ Late winter

The 1BHK Math

I tried doing it the proper way first.

When I moved to Bengaluru three years ago, I rented a 1BHK on my own. It was the version of independent living I'd seen on screens — a small, solo space, a kitchen, a door I could close. What I hadn't seen on the screens was the math. Rent. Then cooking gas. Then electricity that surprised me every month. Then commute, because the place was nowhere near work — and Bengaluru's traffic does not negotiate.

Ubers every day were not a financial plan. So I switched to the bus. Anyone who's done a peak-hour bus in this city in summer knows the temperature of it. You don't ride a Bengaluru bus; you marinate in it. By the time I got home, I had nothing left to cook with.

What I felt most, though, wasn't the cost or the commute. It was the silence. I'd walk into my own apartment and it wasn't a relief. It was the thing pressing on me.

My colleagues at the time — most of them now my closest friends — were almost all in PGs. I'd watch how easy their lives looked. Food appeared. Laundry appeared. The walk to the office was ten minutes. They'd come over for chai and leave for a movie at 9 pm because nothing about getting around their evening was hard. Mine was hard. So I moved.

↳ Spring

The PG, Honestly

I picked an upscale PG near the office. Standalone room — that part mattered, because privacy isn't optional for me — but everything around the room is shared in the way that makes a city feel smaller. Food in the dining hall when I want it. Groceries five minutes away. Bus stop, metro stop, walk to anywhere I actually need to go.

The areas where PGs cluster get something right: they are dense, and they are alive. Yes, the roads are bad. Yes, traffic and noise. But this is the trick of moving into a loud place — for the first few weeks the noise rents space in your head, and after that your head simply files it away. I do not hear the horns anymore. I hear them only when a friend visits and complains about them, and then I notice them again, like a smell in your own kitchen.

The social layer is real. I leave my room and there are people I know. I'm not "networking" — I'm walking past someone whose name I learned over a long weekend, and we say hi, and sometimes it goes somewhere and sometimes it doesn't, and that's how a city actually works.

A 1BHK gave me an apartment. The PG gave me a neighborhood.

↳ Late summer · Harvest

Cooking, And What It Actually Freed

I always knew how to cook in the basic sense. In the 1BHK I cooked, but it was survival cooking — a tired meal at 9 pm I didn't enjoy making and didn't enjoy eating. Cooking like that is just another chore between you and bed.

In the PG, two things changed at once. I had energy left at the end of the day, and I had time. So I started watching videos. I started buying ingredients on purpose, instead of by panic. And then a thing happened I think most people don't expect — cooking became the part of the day I look forward to.

Mostly Italian. I lean European and American at home because the smells are easier to live with in a shared space — you don't want a chili tadka announcing itself to twenty other rooms. Spaghetti aglio e olio is my go-to: garlic, olive oil, chili flakes, pasta water, done in fifteen minutes, and it tastes like someone tried. A creamy mushroom pasta with a white wine sauce when I want to feel a little fancier. Garlic bread, salad, evening done.

I cook Asian when I can — stir-fried noodles, fried rice, tofu (love tofu), tom kha, a few Thai curries, and Indomie's Mi Goreng on the weeks that have been long. Eggs run out faster than anything in my fridge; I once made a special trip to the store for a single carton and learned, mid-aisle, that double-yolk eggs are now a thing you can buy in Bengaluru. Small joy.

People say cooking is meditative. That's true, but it undersells it. Cooking is the place where I get to choose. Choose what's in my body, choose how the evening sounds, choose whether there's wine in the pan. I depend on no one for it.

After a job, GMAT prep, gym, and the world being on fire on the TV, having one thing in the day where I am uncontested in charge — that is the freedom part. It's small, and it's real.

↳ High summer

A Day, Honestly

6:30 am. Up. A jog while the city is still negotiating its mood. Shower, GMAT, coffee — the coffee being non-negotiable. Work begins at 10:30; my schedule is flexible enough that I can be in the office or at home depending on what the day needs.

I'm an AI Innovation Applications Engineer at Airbus, and I get to lead a few aviation projects on the business side too. I write that sentence and still have to remind myself that it's mine. I work on cutting-edge AI in a company that genuinely makes things — heavy, beautiful, complicated things that move people across the world. I get free reign to follow my interests, and to call that work. I am very, very aware of how lucky that is. Touch wood.

Lunch around 1, light, from the office options. Back to it through the afternoon. Then evening GMAT classes. Then home — and home is when the day actually opens. I cook. I eat. I clean up after myself, because nobody else will and an untidy room costs me sleep. Maybe a movie. Maybe a call with someone I miss. Bed by 11 if I'm being honest, by 12:30 if I'm not.

When life gets heavy, sleep is the first thing to slip. It always is. I tell myself it's temporary; I try to keep the rest of it intact — gym, food, friends — because I've learned that the day a few of those go down at once is the day I stop functioning. Sleep I can claw back on a Sunday. The rest, less so.

The GMAT is in the next few months. The plan, unconventionally for a lot of people in my circle, is Asia — Singapore, Hong Kong. The US and Europe are the obvious moves. I am not interested in the obvious move. I want to be in business schools pointed at the markets I think will define the next decade, and I want the friction of a different kind of room than the one most of my peers will be in.

↳ Autumn

Friends, Imperfectly

My friends are scattered. Some are from back home in Hyderabad, who I see when I'm there and call when I miss them. Some are colleagues, who became close because we built things together and learned each other's worst weeks. Some are PG mates, who I drift in and out of because we live next to each other and it's easy.

I am not a great friend in the busy weeks. I'm honest about that. The GMAT and the job and the cooking and the keeping a room together — they take from somewhere, and friendships are quiet enough to not complain when I take from them. The thing I've gotten better at is saying that out loud, and showing up properly when I can.

↳ Hard winter

The World, Loud

I usually try to keep the news at arm's length. This year, that hasn't worked.

In late February the US and Israel hit Iran. Khamenei was killed. Five weeks of war, then a ceasefire that has been more pause than peace. As I write this, the Lebanon truce just got extended for another three weeks, talks with Iran are stalled, the Strait of Hormuz is under blockade, and Israel's defence minister has said publicly they are ready to resume fighting if Washington gives the word. Thousands dead, millions displaced.

Those are the official numbers. You read that, and you stand in your kitchen in Bengaluru, and you make pasta, and you do not know what to do with the gap.

↳ Dusk

Old Songs for New Wars

What I do, mostly, is play old music.

I keep coming back to the '60s through the '90s — Led Zeppelin, Elton John, Pink Floyd, the Stones, AC/DC, Metallica, Nirvana, Guns N' Roses, the Chili Peppers, all the obvious ones. Grunge in particular got me through adolescence into adulthood, and Nirvana especially is a band I owe something to that I won't be able to repay.

But my heavy daily rotation is softer than that — Chicago, REO Speedwagon, the Eagles, the Beatles, Fleetwood Mac. Some country mood weeks I'm deep in Lynyrd Skynyrd; The Ballad of Curtis Loew has been on repeat lately. I feel bad for Curtis. Dire Straits, the Police, Steppenwolf, the Doors — they all rotate.

All of it, in one place. Side A through B-side.

The one song. Landslide by Fleetwood Mac. That's the lyric on repeat right now. I don't have a better explanation for it than that some songs find you at the right age. Stevie Nicks wrote it at twenty-seven, I'm twenty-six, and the line about being afraid of changing because you've built your life around someone — only it isn't a someone for me, it's a city, a stove, a job, a slow-built version of an adult life — that line is louder than anything else this season.

The wartime parallel is that history doesn't actually do anything new. The Cold War sat over my parents' parents under threat of mushroom clouds, and the same decades gave us War Pigs and Sympathy for the Devil — songs that didn't pretend the world was fine. Civil War by Guns N' Roses came later but kept the same line of thought. Those songs were written by people standing exactly where I'm standing now: in a kitchen, with a job, with the news on, knowing the people deciding things are not them and may not have their interests in mind.

Why the music helps isn't that it cheers anything up. It's the opposite — the music acknowledges it. The acknowledgement is the thing. I don't need a song to lie to me; I need a song to say yes, this is what it feels like, you're not crazy, others have been here, and they kept going.

And they kept going. That's the part that matters.

↳ Early spring

Better, Defined Small

I'm 26. I don't have most things figured out. What I do have is enough years behind me to have seen — clearly enough to bet on it — that consistency works. In anything.

So when I say I want to be better every day, I don't mean a self-help thing. I mean exactly what the words say. Better than yesterday. Slightly more patient with the people I care about. Slightly more present in the meal I'm making instead of half-thinking about the next thing. Slightly braver, because the one thing I think my generation is short on is bravery — and a related thing, selflessness. We are all very focused on our own lives. Not all of that is wrong, but some of it is, and I want to spend the next decade being a person who notices when someone else needs something and just does it.

Concretely, this week: cook one new recipe. Sleep before midnight on three of five weekdays. Call one friend I've been meaning to. Get through one extra GMAT problem set. Don't doomscroll past 10 pm. Show up for the people in my building when they need me to.

Life moves on whether or not we're ready. The world will be loud whether or not we asked for that. The only piece of all this we have any actual control over is what we do, today, in the room we're in.

Mine has a stove, and a playlist, and a window. That's enough to start.
